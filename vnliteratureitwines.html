I

An Unexpected Encounter

Premio Nonino & Friuli — Where Literature Drinks Wine

Few people know that the prestigious literary award Premio Nonino — where Vietnamese writer Nguyễn Huy Thiệp was once honoured — is not held in Rome or Milan. It takes place in Friuli-Venezia-Giulia, in the far northeast of Italy, on the borders of Slovenia and Austria. This is the very land that gave birth to the world-famous grappa of the Nonino family.

Since 1975, the Nonino distilling dynasty decided to award a prize to authors "who bring light to human culture." Wine and literature side by side — not by accident. Both are arts of distillation: one draws from grapes, the other from the soul.

✦ Vietalian Perspective

Nguyễn Huy Thiệp on Wine Country Soil

Nguyễn Huy Thiệp received his Premio Nonino award in Friuli itself — where vineyard-covered Collio hillsides produce Italy's most elegant Pinot Grigio and Ribolla Gialla. He stood on that land, surrounded by the scent of wine and the air of international literature.

That was the moment Viet and Italian truly met — not over a meal, but in the depths of culture.

A Tasting Journey

20 Italian Wine Regions — What Vietnamese Wine Lovers Need to Know

Italy has 20 administrative regions, and the magic is that almost all of them produce wine. No country on earth offers the same diversity of grape varieties and wine styles as Italy. Here is the most essential map for Vietnamese explorers just beginning their journey.

🗺 How to Read the Italian Map

Think of Italy as a boot. The North (top of the boot) borders the Alps and the Po Valley — continental climate, structured and powerful wines. Central Italy is where Roman heritage meets rolling hills — Tuscany and Umbria reign here. The South and islands offer more sun, fuller-bodied wines, and often excellent value.

Vietalian Pairing

Drinking Italian Wine the Vietnamese Way — Surprising Pairings

Italians and Vietnamese share one deep truth: both treat eating and drinking as ritual. A family meal in Hanoi or a dinner in Bologna is sacred time — never rushed. That is why Italian wine pairs with Vietnamese cuisine far more naturally than most people expect.

Amarone della Valpolicella (Veneto) — the wine featured on the Vietalian logo — with its high alcohol, firm tannins, and flavours of dried plum and smoked meat, is a natural companion to Phú Quốc-style black pepper beef stew or slow-braised pork belly at Tết. Vermentino from Sardinia, with its bright acidity and almond finish, paired with fresh prawn spring rolls or steamed fish with ginger, is a discovery reserved for the truly curious.

That is the spirit of Vietalian: not fusion for its own sake, but finding the genuine meeting points between two cultures that have both lived through time, learned to love beauty, and discovered how to turn earth into art.
